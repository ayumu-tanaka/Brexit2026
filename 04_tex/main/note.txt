## Revisions to Table 1

I revised the cross-margin equality test in Table 1 to test the difference in the **absolute values** of the entry and exit DiD coefficients, |β_entry| − |β_exit|, rather than the signed difference β_entry − β_exit. The test is based on the delta method applied to a stacked PPML model, using the variance-covariance matrix to compute the standard error.

The updated estimates are: |β_entry| − |β_exit| = 0.294 (SE = 0.118, p = 0.013), indicating that the magnitude of the new-entry decline (−0.298) significantly exceeds the magnitude of the exit increase (+0.004). This asymmetry is consistent with a real-options interpretation: the Brexit referendum deterred new investment more strongly than it accelerated the withdrawal of existing affiliates.

Referee comment:
"1.	The authors interpret the lack of affiliate exit and divestment as driven by increased hysteresis associated with the increase in macroeconomic uncertainty caused by Brexit. While I am sympathetic to this interpretation, the authors have no hard evidence to push this narrative. The same qualitative outcome could be driven by a completely different mechanism. Several papers in the literature frame Brexit as analogous to an increase in trade costs between the UK and continental Europe. While an increase in trade costs would affect the activities of exporters, it is not clear it should affect MNE affiliates. More precisely, if Japanese MNE affiliates are mostly horizontal in scope (as it appears to be the case in the UK), an increase in UK-EU trade costs should not affect their operations. So one could interpret the paper’s empirical finding as simply confirming that the increases in trade costs associated with Brexit did not affect horizontal FDI. One way to disentangle the trade costs channel from the hysteresis channel would be to compare affiliates with different destinations of their sales. While horizontal affiliates’ exit should not be affected by Brexit either via the trade cost channel or via the hysteresis channel, affiliates that sell to third markets (export platform affiliates) should exhibit increased exit from the UK according to the trade cost channel, but not according to the hysteresis channel. In other words, a robustness analysis based on export platform affiliates would be more persuasive of the actual mechanism in place. The authors talk about the role of trade with the EU for interpreting the results at the end of Section 2, but a thorough analysis exploiting information on the destination of affiliate sales is missing."

Mike's Notes on Divestment - studies that use divestment rather than employment reduction and/or sales reduction to measure divestment.
1. UNCTAD World Investment Reports (e.g., 2016, 2018)
How divestment is measured: Changes in foreign direct investment (FDI) positions and flows, specifically negative greenfield or M&A values due to asset sales or closures.
Example: UNCTAD reports often note instances of foreign affiliate divestments, such as when a multinational sells off or shuts down a subsidiary in a host country.
Citation:
UNCTAD. (2016). World Investment Report: Investor Nationality – Policy Challenges. United Nations Conference on Trade and Development.

2. Belderbos & Zou (2006) – "Foreign divestment and relocation by Japanese electronics firms in East Asia"
How divestment is measured: Exit of Japanese foreign affiliates using MITI data (Ministry of International Trade and Industry).
Measurement: Observed closures or ownership transfers of affiliates; direct indicator of divestment from equity stake in subsidiaries.
Citation:
Belderbos, R., & Zou, J. (2006). Foreign divestment and relocation by Japanese electronics firms in East Asia. Asian Economic Journal, 20(1), 1–27.

3. Buch, Kokta, & Piazolo (2003) – "Foreign direct investment in Europe: Is there redirection from the South to the East?"
How divestment is measured: Firm-level affiliate exit data from the Bundesbank MiDi database.
Example: Analyzes how German firms shift FDI from Southern to Eastern Europe by closing or selling existing affiliates.
Citation:
Buch, C. M., Kokta, R. M., & Piazolo, D. (2003). Foreign direct investment in Europe: Is there redirection from the South to the East? Journal of Comparative Economics, 31(1), 94–109.

4. Bertrand, Kramarz, Schoar, & Thesmar (2018) – "The cost of political connections in a low-corruption environment"
How divestment is measured: Sale of politically connected subsidiaries after a change in political regime.
Example: Used data from France to show firms divested (sold off) politically connected subsidiaries after a loss of connection, using firm-level financial and ownership data.
Citation:
Bertrand, M., Kramarz, F., Schoar, A., & Thesmar, D. (2018). The cost of political connections in a low-corruption environment. The Review of Economic Studies, 85(2), 611–646.

5. Javorcik & Wei (2009) – "Corruption and cross-border investment in emerging markets"
How divestment is measured: Exits of foreign affiliates due to corruption risks.
Example: Uses firm-level data to identify affiliate closures or ownership withdrawals as a response to institutional quality.
Citation:
Javorcik, B. S., & Wei, S.-J. (2009). Corruption and cross-border investment in emerging markets. Journal of International Money and Finance, 28(4), 605–624.

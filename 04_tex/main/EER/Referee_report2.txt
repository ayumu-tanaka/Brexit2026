
Reviewer #2: Review of EEREV-D-25-00575: "Anchored Abroad: Sunk Costs, Asset Specificity, and Japanese Affiliate Exit from Brexit Britain"

Summary:
This paper uses data on Japanese multinational affiliates in the UK from 2010 to 2020 and shows that, relative to Japanese affiliates in the rest of the EU, there was no significant change in exit rates among Japanese affiliates in the UK following the Brexit referendum. The authors attribute this to hysteresis generated by the high sunk costs of FDI. As supporting evidence, they show that affiliates in low asset-specificity industries did significantly increase their exit rates post-referendum, but affiliates in high asset-specificity industries did not. Moreover, affiliate entry rates significantly declined.

Evaluation:
The effect of Brexit on FDI decisions is an important and interesting topic. However, I have some concerns about both the empirical and theoretical approaches used in this paper.

Comments:
(1) Dif-in-dif specification. The empirical exercise estimates whether Japanese affiliates that were in the UK before the Brexit referendum had higher post-referendum exit rates relative to those of Japanese affiliates in other high-income EU countries. However, given the complexity of multinational firm structures, it's unclear if this is a valid comparison group. For example, if a Japanese firm had an affiliate in a non-UK EU country prior to 2016, and this affiliate exported heavily to the UK, then the Brexit vote might lead this non-UK affiliate to exit, as well. This could also bias the entry estimates if firms view entering the UK or a non-UK EU country as substitutes, or if the increased cost of trade between the UK and EU impacted the expected value of opening an affiliate in either of these locations. More broadly, Brexit was a shock to the EU, not just to the UK, bringing into question the appropriateness of other EU countries as a valid control group. Also, the list of high-income EU countries that are including in the comparison group seems to include the UK (p.9). Is this correct? If the UK was included in the comparison group, then it should be removed. If it was not included, then the description on page 9 should make this clear.

>  **Partially addressed (April 6, 2026).** The concern about EU countries as a valid control group is addressed in Table 3 ("Effect of the Brexit Referendum on Exit Probability"), which reports results using non-EU high-income countries as the sole comparison group, and results combining EU and non-EU high-income countries. Results are robust across all control group specifications. The text listing the high-income EU comparison countries (previously "Luxembourg, Ireland, Denmark, Netherlands, Sweden, Austria, Germany, Finland, Belgium, France, United Kingdom, and Italy") has been corrected by removing the United Kingdom, making clear that the UK is the treated unit and not part of the comparison group. The potential spillover concern — that the Brexit vote may have also affected non-UK EU affiliates exporting to the UK — has not been directly addressed.

(2) Models of FDI. The theoretical motivation includes no references to the literature on the costs of FDI and on MNE expansion. This is a significant omission. Instead, the model used to motivate the paper is based on the exporting model of Roberts and Tybout (1997). The authors simply state that "However, in comparison to the extensive research on exporting behavior, studies explicitly focusing on the role of sunk costs in FDI decisions are less prevalent. This notable research gap highlights the need for further investigation into this important aspect to enhance our understanding of FDI determinants" (p. 4). But there are plenty of papers out there that focus on the role of sunk costs in FDI decisions, including by the editor handling this submission. See, for example Garetto et al. (2024). As illustrated by point (1) above, FDI differs from exporting in important ways that should be more carefully considered by the authors.

>  **Addressed (April 6, 2026).** Garetto et al. is now cited in a footnote in the model section, acknowledging that their framework characterises MNE expansion patterns in time and space, while positioning our contribution as focused specifically on the exit margin following a large negative policy shock. Tamberi (2024) is also cited in the same footnote, discussing export-platform FDI under trade policy uncertainty. Stefania Garetto is additionally acknowledged in the acknowledgments section. Together, these additions broaden the engagement with the FDI sunk cost literature beyond the Roberts and Tybout (1997) exporting framework.

(3) Timing. The UK did not actually exit the EU until 2020. However, this paper uses data from 2010 to 2020. The authors argue that the Brexit referendum, which took place in 2016, introduced sufficient uncertainty that it was likely to impact firm decisions even before Brexit was officially implemented. Indeed, previous work has shown meaningful economic effects of the 2016 vote. But for the purpose of these paper, which relies on the role of hysteresis and sunk costs, might there be important differences between the effects of uncertainty generated by the vote and the effects of the UK's actual exit from the EU? This should be discussed in the paper. Also, most of the papers that focused on the effect of the vote, rather than the UK's actual exit, did so because they were written shortly after the referendum (e.g. between 2016 and 2020) and there simply was no post-Brexit data because the exit hadn't happened yet. Now that it's been 5 years since the UK left the EU, it would be good to update the data to include post-2020 years. At the very minimum, the authors should be careful about the language they use. For example, this paper does not examine "Brexit's impact on foreign direct investment". It examines the impact of the Brexit vote.

>  **Partially addressed (April 6, 2026).** The language issue is fully addressed: "Brexit" has been replaced throughout by "the Brexit referendum" or "the referendum" wherever the text refers to the June 2016 vote, and a scope clarification was added to the introduction stating that estimates should be interpreted as responses to the referendum and associated policy uncertainty rather than to the post-2021 trading regime. The discussion of potential differences between uncertainty effects (2016–2020) and the effects of the UK's actual exit has also been added. However, the data have not been extended beyond 2020, as post-2020 data from the Toyo Keizai OJC database are not currently available to us.

(4) Importance of Japanese Investment to the UK. The paper should provide more context about the importance of Japanese investment. For example, what share of total inward FDI in the UK comes from Japan? Would we expect firms from other countries to behave the same way as Japanese firms? Is it possible that the sunk costs of investing in the UK are higher or lower for Japanese firms relative to firms from other countries due to their industry mix or factors such as distance, language, culture, etc? How generalizable are the results?

>  **Partially addressed (April 26, 2026).** The introduction now adds context on the importance of Japanese investment using Kox's UIFS4 bilateral inward FDI stock data. It states that Japan was among the top ten source economies for UK inward FDI stocks in every year from 2001 to 2022. In 2016, Japanese FDI stocks in the UK were about USD 114 billion, accounting for 4.1% of numerically reported UK inward FDI stocks; Japan ranked sixth overall and second among non-EU source economies, after the United States. The text also notes that from 2014 onward Japan remained the second-largest non-EU source economy for UK inward FDI stocks. The broader generalizability question--whether firms from other countries would behave similarly, and how distance, language, culture, and industry mix shape sunk costs--is still only partly addressed and may require further discussion.

(5) Intensive margin. This paper looks exclusively at the extensive margins of affiliate entry, exit, and ownership. It would be interesting to also consider the intensive margin effects on outcomes like the volume of sales, number of employees, capital investment, etc. There may be little affiliate exit, but are the affiliates that remain changing the scale of their activities? There is less affiliate entry, but are the affiliates that do enter larger or smaller than those that entered pre-referendum?

>  **Not yet addressed.** Intensive margin outcomes (sales, employment, capital investment) have not been analysed. Note also that the ownership/divestment analysis, which was the closest proxy for an intensive margin in the previous version, has been removed from the paper as part of the scope revision.
